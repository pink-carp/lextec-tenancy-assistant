**LEGAL Information:** 

**The landlord cannot legitimately keep your deposit blocked indefinitely without a valid claim. But after four months, the bank generally cannot release it merely because he is ignoring you.** These are two separate issues under **Art. 257e of the Swiss Code of Obligations (OR)**: whether the landlord has grounds to withhold consent, and whether the bank is authorised to pay out.

On your account—a satisfactory handover and no specific claims notified since—you have a strong basis to demand release now. **You do not have to wait a year before taking action.**

1. **Why the bank is refusing**

Under **Art. 257e OR**, a residential cash deposit must be held in a bank account in the tenant’s name. Three months’ rent is the statutory maximum.

The bank may release the deposit with:

* **both parties’ consent**;  
* a final, enforceable court judgment; or  
* a final, enforceable payment order.

There is also a **one-year fallback**: if the landlord has not legally pursued a claim against you within one year after the tenancy ended, you can demand repayment directly from the bank without his signature. Formal pursuit can include debt-enforcement proceedings or a conciliation application; simply refusing to sign or sending an invoice is not enough.

**The year runs from the legal end of the tenancy, not necessarily the date you moved out.** It is not a blanket permission for the landlord to withhold the deposit for a year.

2. **“I’ll still look everything over” does not preserve damage claims indefinitely**

Under **Art. 267 OR**, you are not responsible for ordinary wear and tear from proper use. Under **Art. 267a OR**, the landlord must inspect the flat on return and immediately notify you of defects for which he considers you responsible. If he fails to do so, he loses claims for defects that a normal inspection would have revealed.

The Zurich Tenancy Court’s decision **MG170019** interprets “immediately” as **within a few working days after return** and places the burden of proving timely notification on the landlord.

A general statement that the manager will inspect later is **not, by itself, notification of any specific defect**. If nothing specific was reported at handover or promptly afterwards, claims for ordinary, visible damage are likely forfeited after four months.

There are two important qualifications:

* **Hidden defects:** defects not discoverable during a normal inspection can still be claimed, but must be reported immediately upon discovery.  
* **Other tenancy debts:** unpaid rent or a valid outstanding service-charge balance are separate from damage claims and are not extinguished by the damage-notification rule.

The handover report and any subsequent notices therefore matter.

3. **What to do now**  
1. **Send a registered letter to the landlord**, copying the property manager. Demand signature of the bank’s release form within **14 days**. Ask him to identify any alleged outstanding claim, its amount, supporting documents and—if it concerns damage—when it was notified to you. The 14 days is a practical deadline you set, not a statutory release period.  
2. **Keep the evidence:** the tenancy agreement, termination confirmation, handover report, photographs, deposit statement and unanswered emails. Include the bank’s release form with your letter.  
3. **If he does not cooperate, apply to the tenancy conciliation authority attached to the Winterthur District Court** for release of the deposit. You can do this now. The Zurich courts’ official guidance identifies the authority for the district where the flat is located as the appropriate starting point. The conciliation stage carries **no court fees** under Art. 113 ZPO; your own lawyer’s fees, if any, are separate. If no settlement is reached, further court proceedings may be necessary.  
4. **Keep track of the one-year date.** If no claim has been legally pursued within that year, ask the bank for repayment under Art. 257e(3) OR and provide evidence of when the tenancy ended.

**Your immediate route is to demand release and, if necessary, seek conciliation—not to keep sending unanswered emails or assume you must wait another eight months.** Your need for the money does not itself override the bank’s release requirements, but the landlord’s silence does not prevent you from pursuing recovery.
