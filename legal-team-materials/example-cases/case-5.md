Summary: 

**A pet ban can be lawful in Switzerland—but management’s statement that you cannot keep any animal at all is too broad.** Your position depends principally on whether the ban forms part of your agreed tenancy terms and whether the cat will be kept entirely indoors. Calling it a mental-support animal does **not, by itself, establish a right to override the ban**.

**My recommendation is to seek written permission while checking the original agreement, rather than bring the cat home in defiance of the refusal.** Your promise to take the cat does not bind the landlord.

1. **Is the house-rule ban binding?**

There is no specific provision in the Swiss Code of Obligations (OR) giving tenants an unconditional right to keep a cat. The starting point is the **agreed contractual use of the flat**. Under **Art. 1 OR**, contractual terms require agreement, which may be express or implied.

* **If the original lease contains the ban, or incorporates house rules containing it:** management has a substantially stronger position. House rules do not necessarily need a separate signature, but they must have become part of your agreement.  
* **If the ban appears only in rules introduced later:** management cannot simply assume that displaying or sending new rules changes your existing tenancy rights. A unilateral change to your disadvantage is subject to **Art. 269d(3) OR**, including the prescribed official form, reasons and notice requirements.  
* **If neither the lease nor incorporated rules restrict pets:** management has a much weaker basis for prohibiting an ordinary, non-disruptive cat.

Your **25 years of residence do not automatically invalidate an existing ban**, but they make it particularly important to check the original terms rather than only today’s house rules.

2. **Cats are a borderline case—not the same as all other pets**

The Swiss Tenants’ Association (*Mieterverband*) distinguishes ordinary, harmless small animals from pets that may be contractually restricted. It identifies **cats as a borderline case** and takes the position that a cat kept entirely inside the flat can generally be treated as an unobjectionable small animal.

That gives you a **credible argument against a blanket ban if this will be one indoor-only cat**, with no disturbance, smell or use of shared areas. It is not a guaranteed outcome. A free-roaming cat, a cat using the communal stairwell, or alterations such as a cat flap raise different issues.

The Swiss Homeowners’ Association (*HEV*) takes a more landlord-favourable position on contractual consent requirements. **I would not present the indoor-cat argument as settled law.** I could not verify the underlying older pet-ban judgment directly in the available decision sources, so this assessment does not rely on an unverified case citation.

3. **Does your need for emotional support change this?**

**Do not assume that the label “emotional-support animal” creates an automatic exemption in a Swiss residential tenancy.** Nor should an ordinary companion cat automatically be equated with a trained disability-assistance animal.

Your bereavement and the cat’s expected benefit are nevertheless important reasons to request an **individual exception**, rather than accept a mechanical refusal. If a treating doctor or therapist considers the cat medically important, a short supporting letter can strengthen that request and any subsequent assessment of your circumstances. It should explain the relevant need without disclosing your entire medical history.

Your long tenancy and recent loss are strong practical grounds for reconsideration, but I cannot conclude from those facts alone that management must consent.
